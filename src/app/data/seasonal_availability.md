# Seasonal Flower Availability

Guidance on when common flowers are in season, why seasonality matters for florists and customers, and how to plan around it. Availability below reflects typical Northern Hemisphere growing patterns and standard commercial floristry supply; imported/greenhouse-grown stock can extend a flower's practical availability year-round at a higher cost.

## Why seasonality matters

Buying in-season flowers is cheaper, fresher, and generally lasts longer in the vase, since the flowers travel shorter distances and spend less time in cold storage before reaching a shop. Out-of-season flowers are usually imported or greenhouse-grown, which raises cost and can slightly reduce vase life due to longer transit times. Seasonality also affects color and variety availability — some cultivars are only grown commercially during their natural season and are hard to source reliably outside it. For weddings and large events booked months in advance, checking a flower's season early avoids late substitutions or price surprises.

## Spring flowers (March–May)

Peak spring flowers include tulips, daffodils, hyacinths, ranunculus, anemones, peonies (late spring), lilacs, and sweet peas. This is the season for soft pastel palettes and garden-style arrangements. Peonies have a notably short natural season (roughly 6–8 weeks) and are heavily in demand for spring/early-summer weddings, so they should be booked well ahead. Tulips and daffodils are bulb flowers and are typically the most affordable, widely available options in early spring.

## Summer flowers (June–August)

Summer brings the widest natural variety: roses, sunflowers, dahlias, zinnias, hydrangeas, delphiniums, lisianthus, stock, and snapdragons all peak in this window. Garden roses and dahlias are especially associated with high summer. This is generally the most flexible season for sourcing a wide color palette locally, and prices for many varieties are at their lowest due to peak supply.

## Fall flowers (September–November)

Fall favorites include chrysanthemums, marigolds, celosia, dahlias (continuing from late summer), amaranthus, and late-season sunflowers. Warm, rich tones (deep orange, burgundy, mustard yellow) dominate natural fall availability. Chrysanthemums are especially abundant and affordable in autumn, making them a practical anchor flower for fall arrangements and events.

## Winter flowers (December–February)

Natural winter availability is the most limited of the four seasons. Amaryllis, poinsettias, holly, paperwhites (narcissus), and hellebores are among the few flowers genuinely in season. Roses, carnations, and lilies remain available year-round via greenhouse/import supply chains and are the practical default for winter arrangements when natural seasonal options are scarce. Valentine's Day (mid-February) drives a major seasonal price spike for roses specifically, independent of natural availability.

## Year-round flowers

Some flowers are commercially available in most markets throughout the year due to global greenhouse production and import networks: roses, carnations, chrysanthemums, alstroemeria, gerbera daisies, and orchids. These are reliable fallback choices when a specific seasonal flower isn't available or when a customer needs a consistent look regardless of month.

## Planning around seasonality

For weddings and large events, confirm flower choices at least 3–4 months ahead if any selections are tightly seasonal (peonies, garden roses, dahlias, lilac). Have a same-color-family backup in mind from the year-round list in case a seasonal flower's supply is disrupted by weather or crop issues close to the event date. For everyday arrangements, leaning into what's currently in season is usually both the most cost-effective and freshest option.

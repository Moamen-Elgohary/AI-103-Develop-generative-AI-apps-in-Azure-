# Flower Handling and Processing

Florist-side techniques for processing cut flowers on arrival and preparing them for arrangement — distinct from the ethylene/sap/water-pairing rules covered in flower compatibility guidance and the vase-life data tracked separately.

## Conditioning on arrival

"Conditioning" is the process of rehydrating and preparing flowers immediately after they arrive from a wholesaler, before they're used in any arrangement. Flowers typically spend time in transit and cold storage, which leaves them mildly dehydrated even if they look fresh. Standard conditioning involves recutting stems underwater or immediately after cutting (to prevent air bubbles from blocking water uptake), removing any foliage that would sit below the waterline, and placing stems in room-temperature water with flower food for several hours to overnight before arranging. Woody or hard stems (hydrangea, lilac, roses) benefit from a longer conditioning period than soft-stemmed flowers (tulips, ranunculus).

## Stem processing techniques

Beyond the standard 45-degree recut, some stems need special treatment to take up water properly. **Woody stems** (hydrangea, lilac, roses with thick canes) benefit from splitting or lightly crushing the last inch of stem to expose more surface area for water uptake. **Hollow stems** (amaryllis, delphinium) can be turned upside down and filled with water, then plugged with a cotton ball, to keep water inside the stem during arranging. **Milky-sap stems** (poppies, euphorbia) benefit from searing the cut end briefly with a flame or dipping in boiling water for a few seconds to seal the sap and prevent it from clogging the stem or contaminating the vase water — this should be done in a well-ventilated area given euphorbia sap's irritant properties.

## Hydration solutions and flower food

Commercial flower food typically contains three components: sugar (feeds the flower), an acidifier (adjusts water pH for better uptake), and a biocide (controls bacterial growth in the water, which is one of the leading causes of premature wilting). Using flower food generally extends vase life meaningfully compared to plain water. A homemade substitute (a small amount of sugar, a few drops of bleach or vinegar, in water) can approximate this but is less reliable than commercial packets. Water should be changed every 2–3 days regardless of flower food use, since bacterial buildup in vase water is a primary driver of early decline independent of nutrition.

## Cold storage and transport

Most cut flowers are best stored in a cooler between roughly 34–40°F (1–4°C) to slow respiration and aging; tropical flowers (orchids, anthuriums, birds of paradise) are the exception and can be damaged by temperatures below about 50°F (10°C), so they should be stored separately at a warmer setting. During transport, flowers should be kept upright in water where possible and shielded from temperature swings, direct sun, and drafts, all of which accelerate water loss and wilting. For events requiring flowers to be prepped a day or more in advance, arrangements are typically stored in a cooler until just before setup to maximize freshness on display.

## Common processing mistakes

Frequent errors that shorten a flower's usable life include: cutting stems with dull or dirty shears (crushes stem tissue and introduces bacteria), leaving foliage below the waterline (accelerates bacterial growth), skipping flower food, letting flowers sit dry out of water for extended periods between conditioning and arranging, and mixing ethylene-sensitive flowers near ripening produce during storage — a handling issue distinct from the vase-pairing guidance covered elsewhere, since it applies even before flowers reach the vase.

# Bouquet Design Principles

Core principles florists use to design balanced, visually appealing bouquets and arrangements — flower roles, proportion, shape, and color.

## Flower roles: focal, filler, and foliage

Most bouquets are built from three functional roles rather than random flower selection. **Focal flowers** are the largest, most visually dominant blooms (roses, peonies, dahlias, hydrangeas) that draw the eye and anchor the design — typically 3–5 stems in a mixed bouquet. **Filler flowers** are smaller, more numerous blooms that add texture and fill visual gaps between focal flowers (baby's breath, statice, waxflower, limonium). **Foliage/greenery** provides structure, backdrop, and volume without competing for attention (eucalyptus, ferns, ruscus, seeded eucalyptus). A well-balanced bouquet typically uses roughly 20% focal, 30% filler, and 50% foliage/greenery by stem count, though ratios vary by style — a lush garden-style bouquet uses more filler, while a modern minimalist design may drop filler almost entirely in favor of a few focal stems and structural greenery.

## Proportion and balance

The bouquet's overall size should be proportional to its context — a bridal bouquet is generally sized to complement the dress and the bride's frame rather than overwhelm it, while a centerpiece needs to work at table height without blocking sightlines across the table. A common guideline is the "golden ratio" approach: the tallest stems should be roughly 1.5 times the height of the container or handle for a balanced silhouette. Balance can be symmetrical (mirrored, formal, common in traditional round bouquets) or asymmetrical (organic, modern, common in cascading or hand-tied garden styles) — asymmetry requires more deliberate weight distribution so the arrangement doesn't look lopsided.

## Shape styles

**Round bouquets** are the classic dome shape, dense and symmetrical, popular for weddings — flowers of similar height are arranged in a tight spiral. **Cascading bouquets** trail downward with graduated stem lengths, creating a waterfall effect — traditionally formal and dramatic, common in bridal work. **Hand-tied bouquets** have a natural, garden-gathered look with visible stems below the binding point, less structured than a round bouquet. **Structured/architectural arrangements** use clean lines and often floral foam or grids, common in modern or corporate settings where precision matters more than organic texture.

## Color theory for bouquets

**Monochromatic** palettes use varying shades and textures of a single color family — elegant and cohesive, forgiving for beginners. **Complementary** palettes pair opposite colors on the color wheel (e.g. purple and yellow, orange and blue) for high contrast and vibrancy. **Analogous** palettes use adjacent colors on the wheel (e.g. pink, red, orange) for a harmonious, blended look. **Triadic** palettes use three evenly spaced colors (e.g. red, yellow, blue) for a bold, balanced result that's harder to execute subtly. White and green are near-universal neutrals that can bridge almost any palette and are commonly used to soften bolder color combinations.

## Texture and visual interest

Beyond color and shape, mixing textures — smooth petals against feathery filler, structured foliage against soft blooms — keeps a bouquet from looking flat. Varying flower forms (round blooms like roses, spiky forms like snapdragons or delphiniums, airy forms like baby's breath) within one arrangement adds depth even within a tight color palette. A common beginner mistake is using only one flower form and one bloom size, which tends to look uniform and less dynamic than a mix.

# Flower Compatibility for Bouquets

Guidance on which flowers pair well together and which combinations shorten a bouquet's life. Based on established floristry/postharvest science.

## Why some flowers don't mix

Three main biological factors affect flower compatibility in a shared vase: ethylene sensitivity, sap toxicity, and mismatched water needs. Each is covered in its own section below.

## Ethylene sensitivity

Ethylene is a natural plant hormone that speeds up aging — it causes wilting, petal drop, and premature fading. Some flowers produce a lot of ethylene as they age; others are highly sensitive to it and decline quickly when exposed.

- **High ethylene producers**: carnations, mature lilies, ripening fruit (never place bouquets near a fruit bowl)
- **Highly ethylene-sensitive flowers**: roses (especially spray roses), snapdragons, gerbera daisies, anemones, delphiniums, dahlias, bouvardia
- **Classic bad pairing**: roses + carnations — the carnations' ethylene output can cause the roses to fade early. Better to keep roses solo or paired with greenery.
- **Classic bad pairing**: lilies + fruit — nearby ripening fruit accelerates lily aging.
- **Reliable, low-sensitivity "anchor" flowers**: chrysanthemums, gerbera daisies (low ethylene output, though moderately sensitive), statice, protea, sunflowers, alstroemeria, lisianthus — these tend to be forgiving in mixed arrangements and are good choices to build a bouquet around when combining several flower types.

## Sap toxicity

Some flowers release a thick, sometimes toxic, sap or slime when their stems are cut. This sap can clog the water-uptake tubes (xylem) of other flowers in the same vase, causing them to wilt even if they were otherwise fresh. Some saps are also irritating to skin.

- **Sap/mucilage-releasing flowers**: daffodils/narcissus, hyacinths, tulips (mild mucilage), amaryllis, poppies, euphorbia-family stems
- **Toxic to handle**: amaryllis and daffodil sap can irritate skin; poppy sap is a mild irritant — wear gloves when processing large quantities
- **Best practice**: keep sap-releasing flowers in a "monobouquet" (their own vase) or let them sit alone in water for a few hours after cutting before combining with other flowers, to let the sap dissipate. Rinse stem ends before adding to a mixed arrangement.

## Water needs

Different flowers have different water depth and temperature preferences. Mismatched needs in the same vase can shorten life for one or both types.

- **Deep, cool water preference**: hydrangeas (soft/woody stems, thirsty)
- **Shallow water preference**: gerbera daisies, bulb flowers
- **General rule**: group flowers with similar water needs together, or use floral foam/separate water sources within a single arrangement if mixing is necessary.

## Safe pairing principles

1. **Match vase life** — pair flowers with similar expected lifespans so the whole bouquet fades evenly, not one type dying while others are still fresh.
2. **Match water needs** — group deep-water and shallow-water flowers separately where possible.
3. **Use greenery as a buffer** — foliage like eucalyptus doesn't carry the same ethylene/sap conflicts and helps visually and structurally separate sensitive blooms.
4. **Isolate sap-releasers** — let daffodils/hyacinths condition alone before adding to a mixed arrangement.
5. **Watch fruit and produce nearby** — keep finished bouquets away from fruit bowls, which emit ethylene.

## General cut-flower care

The "Triple-C" rule for extending vase life:

- **Clean**: use a sterilized vase; bacteria is a leading cause of early decline.
- **Cold/Cool**: keep arrangements away from direct sunlight, heaters, and drafts; use cool water for most cut flowers (bulb flowers prefer lukewarm water).
- **Cut**: recut stems at a 45-degree angle every couple of days to keep water uptake open.
